1. Executive Summary
FireSense AI is proposed as an end-to-end computer-vision research prototype that detects visible fire and 
smoke in live webcam streams and uploaded videos, provides visual evidence, activates a local audible alarm, 
and queues an email notification. A software-only response simulation will illustrate how a confirmed 
detection event can trigger a virtual sprinkler and lower a simulated fire-intensity indicator over time. This 
simulation supports system demonstration and policy experiments, but it does not predict real fire growth, 
sprinkler hydraulics, or extinguishment outcomes.
The baseline engineering task—training a fire/smoke detector and wiring it to an alarm—is useful but may be 
too generic for a strong research paper. Video-based detection is mature, datasets exist, recent reviews 
describe 100+ studies and multiple datasets, and recent papers already address track-level temporal alarm 
validation and multimodal sensor fusion [1–4]. Accordingly, the R&D contribution should not be claimed as 
“the first AI fire detector” or “the first temporal fire detector.”
The recommended research direction is a response-aware evaluation framework: calibrate alarm decisions at 
the event level using detector confidence, evidence persistence, region evolution and an explicit cost of false 
alarms; preserve an audit trail of why an alert fired; and connect confirmed events to a transparent virtual 
suppression simulation. Evaluate the detection policy and simulation policy separately, then together. This 
gives the paper a testable contribution rather than a list of integrated features.
Recommended datasets include D-Fire for accessible initial training, FireAndSmoke and LFDN for difficult 
negatives and broader visual variation, and video datasets such as MIVIA for event-level testing. MmodalFire 
is a recent 2026 dataset that pairs continuous video with physical sensor measurements; it can support a later 
multimodal extension if access, licensing and annotation formats suit the project [5–9]. Split by source 
video/camera/source collection rather than randomly splitting near-duplicate frames.
Expected six-month outcome  A reproducible trained detector, live/upload inference, event-level alert 
policy, secure email notification, browser-based virtual response simulation, a cross-dataset evaluation 
with hard-negative testing, and a conference-ready manuscript draft. Acceptance is not guaranteed.
2. Project Definition and Requirements
2.1 Problem statement
Frame-level visual fire detectors can respond to visually confusing patterns such as sunlight, reflections, 
clouds, mist, steam, illuminated red/orange objects and scene changes. A high frame-wise detection score 
alone does not establish a persistent fire event. Research reviews call out data scarcity, scene dependence, and 
the need to evaluate realistic camera scenarios rather than only image-level accuracy [1,2].

The project therefore targets a connected but separable pipeline: (a) detect and localize fire/smoke; (b) decide

2.2 Primary objectives
 Build a training and evaluation pipeline for a fire/smoke object detector using public, appropriately 
licensed image/video data.
 Support local webcam inference and uploaded video processing, with rendered bounding boxes, class 
labels, confidence scores and timestamps.
 Design a separate event-level alarm gate that can balance detection sensitivity against false alarms and 
delay.
 Implement a local audible alarm and email notification with secure credentials, retry handling, event 
snapshots and a deduplication policy.
 Implement a self-contained software simulation: confirmed fire event  virtual sprinkler opens  → →
simulated severity decreases according to documented parameters.
 Evaluate detection, event alerting, inference performance, hard-negative behaviour and simulation policy 
under controlled scenarios.
 Prepare reproducible experiment logs and a research paper with clear limitations and no fabricated 
results.

3.2 What existing work means for the proposed contribution
The existence of STV-FSANet (August 2026) is particularly important: it directly addresses video-track 
temporal validation of fire/smoke alarms and false detections [4]. Recent 2026 work also studies video 
combined with physical sensors [5]. Therefore, “combine YOLO with temporal filtering,” “reduce false 
alarms,” or “add sensor fusion” are not defensible novelty claims by themselves.
The safer and stronger research position is to propose a specific, measurable, response-aware protocol: an 
event-level decision policy whose thresholds are selected on validation data using a stated false-alarm budget 
and detection-delay constraint, paired with an explainable virtual response simulation that records detection-
to-response timing and scenario-dependent simulated suppression. The distinctive contribution would be the 
experimental formulation and evaluation of this detection-to-response loop—not the mere fact of sending an 
email or animating water.

4. Research Gap, Questions and Hypotheses
4.1 Candidate gap
Public research covers frame-level and video-level fire/smoke detection, temporal verification, difficult 
negatives, and multimodal sensing. A promising student-scale gap to investigate is how an event-level decision 
policy can be calibrated under a target false-alert budget and then evaluated in a transparent response 
simulation that explicitly measures the impact of detection confidence, confirmation delay, fire-region 
location and virtual sprinkler coverage. This is a candidate gap from the initial review, not a claim that no 
prior product or paper has ever implemented a similar idea.
4.2 Research questions
1. RQ1: How does event-level evidence gating change the trade-off between fire-event recall, false alarms per 
hour and time-to-alarm compared with a frame-only threshold?
2. RQ2: How much does training/evaluation with hard-negative sources (e.g., LFDN or FireAndSmoke) change 
false positives on held-out camera/video sources?
3. RQ3: In a defined virtual-response simulation, how do detection delay, sprinkler-zone overlap and virtual 
actuation delay affect simulated residual severity and simulated time-to-extinguish?
4. RQ4: Can the system provide useful visual evidence and audit logs while maintaining acceptable processing 
latency on the available local machine?

5. Proposed Contribution and Novelty Claim Discipline
5.1 Working method name
Response-aware Fire Event Verification and Virtual Suppression Evaluation (ReFiVE). This is a provisional 
name for the proposed method; perform a name search before public use.
5.2 Candidate contributions
 An event-level alert policy that keeps frame detection and alarm confirmation as separate modules, 
exposes the reasons for an alert, supports confidence calibration, hysteresis and a defined alert cooldown.
 A source-wise hard-negative evaluation protocol covering distractors such as clouds, fog, glare, sunlight, 
red/orange objects and illumination changes.
 A simulation-in-the-loop experiment where each confirmed event drives a virtual sprinkler state machine 
and a transparent fire-intensity proxy, allowing controlled measurement of alert delay and simulated 
residual severity.
 A reproducible comparison among frame-only detection, detection plus event gating, and event gating 
plus virtual-response policy; include ablations so the contribution is measurable.
5.3 What should not be claimed
 Do not say “first ever,” “not available in any system,” or “unique worldwide” without a systematic 
literature and product/patent search.
 Do not claim temporal verification is novel: STV-FSANet already studies track-level temporal fire/smoke 
alarm verification [4].
 Do not claim multimodal sensor fusion is novel: MmodalFire and recent sensor-fusion research exist [5]. 
Keep the primary scope vision-only unless sensor data is genuinely available.
 Do not call the virtual fire reduction real suppression, validated fire physics, or evidence that sprinklers 
would extinguish the same event.
5.4 Novelty validation search plan
Search IEEE Xplore, Scopus/Web of Science (if institutionally available), ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect, 
SpringerLink, Google Scholar, Crossref and patent databases. Search strings should include: (fire OR smoke) 
AND (video OR vision) AND (false alarm OR alarm validation OR temporal verification); (fire detection) AND 
(sprinkler OR suppression OR response policy OR digital twin); (fire detection) AND (risk calibration OR event-
level evaluation OR cost-sensitive threshold); and equivalent terms for integrated fire monitoring products. 
Record date, query, filters, screened abstracts, full-text decisions and the closest competing work.

6. Proposed System Architecture
Figure 1. Proposed FireSense architecture. The simulator is a software-only module and the safety boundary prohibits direct physical 
suppression control.
6.1 Functional flow
1. Input: open local webcam or accept an uploaded clip. Preserve source ID, time and file metadata. Never 
upload private camera footage without permission.
2. Preprocess: decode frames, resize consistently, preserve timestamps and record dropped frames. For long 
clips, optionally sample frames for offline evaluation but use continuous streaming for temporal 
evaluation.
3. Detect: run the trained fire/smoke object detector and produce class, confidence and bounding boxes.
4. Associate: link candidate boxes across adjacent frames using a tracking method or simple overlap 
association. Record region persistence, size variation and per-frame confidence.
5. Confirm event: apply a validation-calibrated state machine. Fire/smoke thresholds can differ; retain a fast 
path for very-high-confidence evidence to avoid excessive delay.
6. Alert: immediately raise local software alarm after confirmation, save an evidence snapshot, create one 
event ID, and queue an email. A failed email must not silence the local alarm.
7. Simulate response: send a simulated suppression command to the virtual zone. Track virtual valve state, 
water effect, coverage and the intensity proxy separately from detector output.
8. Log and evaluate: record each candidate and state transition, alert time, email status, simulated actuation 
time, model version, and final experiment label.

7.2 Split policy — essential for credible results
 Create a source manifest containing source dataset, original video ID/URL where allowed, scene, camera, 
licence, class, annotation format and split.
 Split by video/source or scene, not randomly by extracted frames. Adjacent frames and near-duplicates 
must stay in one split.
 Use train/validation/test separation: train for fitting, validation for hyperparameters and thresholds, test 
once for final reporting. Preserve source-wise external tests.
 If combining datasets, identify duplicate images with hashes/perceptual hashes and remove cross-split 
duplicates. Keep a dataset overlap report.
 Create a hard-negative set explicitly labelled by phenomenon: clouds, fog, steam, glare, sunlight, 
red/orange moving object, welding-like light (only from licensed clips), camera motion and compression 
artefacts.
 Record licensing and attribution. Do not redistribute datasets or extracted frames unless the terms permit 
redistribution.
7.3 Labelling policy
Use classes `fire` and `smoke` for localization. Keep images/videos with no event as negative samples with no 
boxes. Define whether smoke boxes include translucent edges, how to label multiple independent regions, and 
how tiny/distant flame patches are treated. Have a second annotator inspect a subset and record agreement or 
disagreement. Video-level event labels should include event start/end time, first visible evidence, fire vs 
smoke, hard-negative category, and whether the event is ambiguous.
8. Model Training and Inference Methodology
8.1 Baseline
Begin with a small real-time object detector such as an appropriately licensed YOLO-family nano/small model, 
then compare with a second model family if hardware/time permit. Fine-tuning from existing general object-
detection weights is a pragmatic baseline. If the research explicitly requires training from random 
initialization, treat that as a separate experiment because it generally needs more data and compute. The 
paper must state pretraining, class map, input size, framework version, and licence.

8.2 Training protocol
1. Inspect and render annotations before training; run label validation and class-count reports.
2. Deduplicate and split sources before extracting frames or augmentations.
3. Use train/validation/test manifests with immutable IDs. Fit with training set only; choose threshold and 
checkpoints on validation only.
4. Augmentation: moderate brightness/contrast, blur, scale, crop, compression and camera-like perturbations. 
Do not use transformations that change the semantic label or erase a tiny fire object.
5. Run an initial pilot to confirm labels, GPU use and loss convergence; then freeze a reproducible 
configuration for full runs.
6. Log training loss, validation precision/recall/mAP, learning rate, seed, epoch, model hash, runtime and 
hardware.
7. Export the best validation checkpoint; test cross-source generalization without fine-tuning on the test 
source.
8.3 Event-level logic
Let a detector generate candidate i at time t with class c, confidence p(t), and box b(t). The event gate uses 
several frames to avoid emitting an email for every transient detection. A simple baseline is a rolling hit rule: 
candidate is positive when p(t)  τ; confirm if at least k of the last n frames are positive. A richer research ≥
policy may add a smoothed score, track persistence, box-area evolution, and uncertainty. Every threshold 
must be tuned using validation data and evaluated against the same locked test set.
Illustrative exponentially smoothed confidence:  s_t = λ s_(t 1) + (1 λ) p_t. A separate persistence score is P_t − −
= positives in last n frames / n. A candidate event score can be defined as R_t = w_s s_t + w_p P_t + w_g G_t  −
w_u U_t, where G_t is a normalized region-growth cue and U_t is a selected uncertainty term. This formula is a 
research candidate, not a validated model. Ablate each component; do not choose weights on test data.

9. Event-Level Alert and Email Design
9.1 Local alarm
After an event is confirmed, trigger the local application sound immediately and show a clear persistent red 
banner. Provide a manual mute/acknowledge option, but record it and do not equate mute with “fire cleared.” 
When a sound output is unavailable, the visual alert and event log must continue. Keep the system heartbeat 
and camera disconnected/error states visible.
9.2 Email event payload
Field Purpose
Event ID / UTC and local timestamp Supports deduplication and event audit.
Camera ID / source clip name Tells the recipient which source generated the event.
Class and confidence Fire/smoke class, event score and the decision rule/version.
Evidence snapshot / short clip reference Allows the recipient to inspect the event; apply retention and access controls.
System status Local alarm state, email delivery state and whether this is a test/simulation event.

9.3 Email reliability and security
 Use a background task/queue so network delays do not block video inference.
 Store SMTP/provider credentials in environment variables or a secret manager, never hard-code them in 
source code or screenshots.
 Use retry with backoff, delivery status, and alert deduplication. Do not promise “instant” delivery; 
measure queue and provider latency.

10. Virtual Fire and Sprinkler Simulation
10.1 Purpose and boundaries
The requested simulation demonstrates a software control flow: when the AI event gate confirms a fire, a 
virtual sprinkler valve opens, water animation appears, and a fire-intensity proxy decreases. It is a 
demonstrator for the application and an experimental environment for comparing response policies. It is not 
a computational fluid dynamics (CFD) model, hydraulic calculation, sprinkler certification, or evidence of real 
extinguishment.

application or a clearly marked manual test trigger. The simulator then sets S_t to `open`; at each simulation 
step it subtracts the virtual water effect scaled by the coverage factor. When intensity reaches a chosen low 
threshold for a preset number of steps, display “simulated extinguishment.” The UI should keep the initial fire 
score, activation time, end score and all assumptions in the audit log.
10.4 Experiments supported by the simulator
 Baseline A: detector output shown, but no virtual response; record the severity proxy trajectory.
 Baseline B: sprinkler opens on the first candidate frame; measure simulated residual severity but count 
false activations on hard negatives.
 Policy C: sprinkler opens only after event confirmation; compare false activations and simulated response 
delay.
 Policy D: confirmation plus virtual coverage factor; compare scenarios with central vs edge-of-zone 
detections and different actuation delays.
 Sensitivity test: vary growth, delay, coverage and water-effect parameters. Publish the ranges and do not 
calibrate them against real-world claims unless validated fire data are available.
Safety separation  Do not connect the prototype to a real water valve, pump, sprinkler control panel, 
building-management system or fire-alarm loop. If hardware is later demonstrated, use LEDs/buzzer and a 
low-voltage mock actuator on a supervised dry bench. Real fire suppression requires qualified engineers, 
certified equipment, site-specific design and applicable standards.

12.1 Evaluation must be conducted at two levels
Detection evaluation asks whether boxes locate fire/smoke. System evaluation asks whether the whole system 
creates useful alerts without spamming, how long the alert takes, and what happens in the response 
simulation. Do not use frame-wise mAP as the only result for a video alarm system.

12.2 Baselines and ablations
 B0: frame-level detector and single threshold.
 B1: detector + k-of-n persistence gate.
 B2: detector + tracking/temporal score + validation-calibrated threshold.
 B3: B2 + hard-negative training data.
 B4: B2 connected to virtual response simulator (policy-only comparison).
 Ablations: remove persistence, remove growth cue, change threshold calibration, change cooldown, 
disable region/coverage factor, vary fire/smoke thresholds.
 Cross-source test: train on one or more sources and test on an unseen dataset/source. Report any 
taxonomy mismatch or excluded class.

12.3 Statistical hygiene
 Use source-level splits and keep the final test data locked.
 Run multiple seeds where practical; report mean and standard deviation or confidence intervals, not only 
the best run.
 When comparing systems, use the same test clips, event definitions and hardware; include the exact 
threshold-selection rule.
 Predefine incident matching: one predicted event may match at most one ground-truth event; specify the 
matching time window and overlap criteria.
 Report negative results, failure cases, dataset imbalance, and differences between image and event 
metrics.
 Do not call simulated severity a physical outcome or report simulated extinction as a real-world success.

14. Risk, Ethics and Safety Controls
Risk Impact Mitigation / required evidence
False negative or late detection A genuine event may be missed or reported too late. Do not deploy as sole safety control; test distant/small fire, occlusion, low light and smoke-only cases; state limits clearly.
False positive from visual confounder Unnecessary alarm/email and virtual sprinkler activation. Hard-negative test suite; validation-calibrated threshold; track/cooldown; report false alarms/hour.
Training/test leakage Inflated results from near-duplicate frames. Source/video/camera-level split and duplicate audit.
Network/email failure Notification does not reach recipients. Local alarm independent of email; retry status; error visibility; delivery tests.
Model/library licence conflict Restrictions on sharing code or weights. Review the exact model, code and dataset licences before publication or distribution.
Privacy breach Camera footage could contain people or sensitive environments. Consent, local processing where possible, access control, minimization, configurable retention and secure evidence links.
Unsafe hardware integration Experimental prototype could activate a real actuator unexpectedly. Simulation-only by default; no real valve or pump integration; physical demo limited to LED/buzzer/mock low-voltage outputs.
Simulation misinterpreted as real physics Results might be overstated as fire suppression. Label all charts “simulated proxy”; document assumptions; exclude simulation from real fire-extinguishment claims.
Unsubstantiated novelty claim Paper rejected or credibility harmed.

17. Conclusion and R&D Recommendation
FireSense AI should be built in two tracks. The engineering track creates a stable local application that accepts 
webcam or uploaded videos, detects and localizes fire/smoke, produces a local alarm and email event, and 
records a visible audit trail. The research track tests a specific response-aware event decision policy using 
source-wise hard negatives and event-level metrics, then uses the virtual sprinkler simulation to perform 
controlled, clearly labelled policy experiments.
The most important improvement over a routine implementation is a disciplined evaluation: real source-held-
out video testing, false alarms per hour, time-to-alarm, cross-dataset robustness, confidence calibration and 
ablations. The virtual simulation adds an engaging closed-loop demonstration and supports reproducible 
policy experiments, but must not be presented as real fire dynamics. Novelty remains provisional until the 
complete related-work and product/patent review is finished.
Recommended next action  In Month 1, prepare the closest-work matrix for [1], [2], [4], [5] and [6]; verify 
dataset terms; select the detector/version and freeze evaluation rules. Do not build the sprinkler 
simulation before defining how a fire event is confirmed and how the resulting states are logged.

Appendix A — Event Gate Reference Logic
The kit includes `appendix/alert_policy_reference.py` as a small readable reference implementation of a k-of-n 
decision gate. It intentionally does not activate hardware, send email, or claim certification. The numerical 
threshold is a placeholder that must be calibrated on validation data. In the final implementation, use 
separate policies for fire and smoke, add a very-high-confidence fast path only if validated, and keep the 
actual event state/log independent from the UI.
Appendix B — Simulator Equation and Interpretation
The kit includes `appendix/simulation_model_reference.py` and `simulation/fire_response_simulation.html`. 
The HTML simulator runs locally in a browser without installation. It demonstrates a test fire, repeated-
evidence confirmation, a virtual alarm, virtual water animation and a simulated severity reduction. Its 
parameters are pedagogical. The model omits fuel type, heat release rate, ventilation, plume dynamics, 
sprinkler activation temperature, droplet size, water flow/pressure, obstruction, water supply and room 
geometry, so no physical inference is valid.
